\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
% \TODO{Maybe add a summary here? Like: In this section, we conduct experiments to answer the following questions: 1, 2, 3..}

We design experiments to validate our central claim—that effective visual tool use is learned from instructive trajectories rather than merely successful ones—and to answer the following questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Effectiveness:} Does fine-tuning on OpenVisTool-42K teach models to effectively leverage visual tools, and can it lift open-source models to closed-source performance levels? (\S\ref{sec:main_results})
    
    \item \textbf{Generalization:} Does the learned tool-use capability transfer to out-of-distribution visual tasks unseen during training, rather than overfitting to domain-specific tool-calling patterns? (\S\ref{sec:ood})
    
    \item \textbf{Observation utility:} Does our filtering strategy (correctness $\cap$ positive tool-use gain) produce stronger training signal than naive SFT on the same queries?(\S\ref{sec:ablation_gain})
    
    \item \textbf{Cross-domain synergy:} Does visual tool use emerge as a transferable meta-skill across domains, and does multi-domain training resolve strategy conflicts that single-domain training introduces? (\S\ref{sec:cross_domain})
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

% \subsubsection{Student Models and Training}

\paragraph{Benchmarks.}
We primarily evaluate on \benchname, which covers diverse visual tool-use scenarios across multiple domains (\ref{sec:bench}). To assess out-of-distribution generalization, we additionally test on Agentic-MME and VTC-Bench—two benchmarks unseen during training. We also report results on the full (unfiltered) Chart and Table source benchmarks (CharXiv, ChartMuseum, TableVQA-Bench, MMTBench) to confirm that gains are not artifacts of our data filtering (Appendix~\ref{app:fullset}).

\paragraph{Base Models and Training.}
We fine-tune four base models spanning two model families and 4B--27B scale --- Qwen3.5-4B/9B/27B and Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct --- on \dataset for three epochs with ms-swift, tools enabled at inference. Optimization details are in the appendix.

% \subsubsection{Inference Protocol and Baselines}

% \paragraph{Protocol.}

\paragraph{Inference Protocol.}
``With tool'' runs the fine-tuned model as a tool-using agent over the shared visual toolset (up to a fixed tool-round budget); ``w/o tool'' runs the same backbone single-pass without tools. Metric is per-domain accuracy and the average, at avg@$k$.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
% Three groups: (i) the \emph{no-tool} base backbones; (ii) released visual tool-use models \textbf{Thyme} and \textbf{DeepEyesV2}, both on Qwen2.5-VL-7B --- a base mismatch with our students, so we frame this as a comparison to \emph{released models}, not a controlled comparison \TODO{Optionally train \dataset on Qwen2.5-VL-7B for an apples-to-apples row.}; and (iii) data-variant baselines (correctness-only, tool-gain-only, and a no-tool-trajectory SFT on the same queries), defined here and used in \S\ref{sec:ablation_gain}.

We compare against three groups:
(i)~the \emph{no-tool} base models \emph{before fine-tuning}, measuring raw tool-use gain;
(ii)~released visual tool-use models (\textbf{Thyme} and \textbf{DeepEyesV2}, both on Qwen2.5-VL-7B), serving as reference points from existing systems rather than controlled comparisons due to backbone mismatch;
and (iii)~data-variant ablations (correctness-only, tool-gain-only, and no-tool-trajectory SFT on the same queries), isolating the contribution of each training signal (\S\ref{sec:ablation_gain}).

\subsection{Main Results}
\label{sec:main_results}
Table~\ref{tab:main} summarizes performance across all models and domains on both in-domain and out-of-distribution benchmarks. We highlight several key findings below.

% \paragraph{\dataset improves every backbone and every domain.}
\paragraph{Consistent gains across all base models and domains.}
% The average rises for all four backbones (Qwen3.5-4B $30.8\!\to\!41.5$, 9B $32.6\!\to\!45.8$, 27B $38.8\!\to\!49.3$, Qwen3-VL-8B $24.0\!\to\!32.9$), across two families and 4B--27B scale.
Our method yields substantial improvements over the no-tool baselines across all domains and model scales. Across the Qwen series ranging from 4B to 27B, fine-tuning with \dataset{} brings an average gain of 10.7 points on the in-domain benchmarks, with the most pronounced improvement observed in VisualSearch (+23.8 points). Notably, even without any fine-tuning, simply equipping closed-source models with our toolset provides significant benefits: GPT-5.5 and Kimi K2.6 achieve 7.1 and 14.1 absolute gains, respectively, demonstrating the inherent utility of the designed tools. However, for smaller open-source models, naively providing tools without dedicated training yields marginal or even negative returns—Qwen3.5-9B, for instance. After fine-tuning on \dataset, these same models learn to leverage tools effectively, comparable to closed-source models. For example, Qwen3.5-9B with our training achieves an average of 45.8, surpassing GPT-5.5's 41.9 without tools and closely approaching its tool-augmented performance of 49.0.



% \paragraph{Gains concentrate where tools surface new evidence.}
\paragraph{Largest gains emerge where tools unlock new visual evidence.}
% Largest improvements are on the evidence-acquisition domains, Visual Search and Table (Qwen3.5-9B: $+23.8$ and $+17.3$); domains answerable from a static view gain least. (Why: \S\ref{sec:cross_domain}.)
% The most significant improvements are concentrated in domains where correct tool use helps the model attend to fine-grained details that are otherwise difficult to capture from a single static view. Specifically, VisualSearch and Table exhibit the largest gains (e.g., Qwen3.5-9B: +23.8 and +14.1, respectively), as tools enable the model to zoom in, crop, or reformat visual content to expose critical evidence. In contrast, domains where the answer is already accessible from the original image receive comparatively modest improvements. We provide a detailed cross-domain analysis in \S\ref{sec:cross_domain}..
For domains where the answer is largely derivable from surface-level visual content—such as Chart, which primarily tests text and semantic extraction from images—a sufficiently capable model can already perform well without tools (e.g., GPT-5.5 scores 63.3 on Chart without tools). However, for more challenging domains involving dense details and hidden evidence—such as VisualSearch, which demands fine-grained spatial and geometric reasoning—models often struggle to succeed with their inherent visual perception alone. In these scenarios, invoking auxiliary tools to crop, zoom, or reformat visual content becomes essential rather than optional. This explains why our method yields the most substantial gains precisely in these demanding domains (e.g., Qwen3.5-9B: +23.8 points on VisualSearch), where tools effectively bridge the gap between what the model can perceive and what the task requires. A detailed cross-domain analysis is provided in \S\ref{sec:cross_domain}.



% \paragraph{Released tool-use models do not transfer cross-domain.}
% On the same shared base, Thyme ($18.2$) and DeepEyesV2 ($15.5$) fall \emph{below} the no-tool base ($19.2$) on the five-domain average, collapsing on out-of-specialty domains (DeepEyesV2: Web2HTML $2.4$, GUI $1.7$) --- motivating a single cross-domain corpus rather than narrow-domain data.
\paragraph{Cross-domain generalization separates \dataset from prior works.}
Existing released models, Thyme and DeepEyes V2, perform worse on the five-domain average than vanilla base models \emph{without any tool access}. Trained on narrow-domain data, these models collapse catastrophically on out-of-specialty tasks, revealing that domain-specific tool-use training fails to generalize. In contrast, models trained with \dataset{} improve uniformly across all five domains, demonstrating that a diverse, multi-domain training corpus is the key to robust and generalizable visual tool use.

\subsection{Out-of-Distribution Generalization}
\label{sec:ood}

\paragraph{Gains hold on benchmarks we did not select.}
To verify that the tool-use capability learned from our five training domains (Chart, GUI, Table, VisualSearch, and Web2HTML) is not domain-specific, we evaluate on two held-out benchmarks—Agentic-MME and VTC-Bench—that cover substantially different visual scenarios unseen during training. As shown in Tab.~\ref{tab:main}, \dataset{}-trained models consistently outperform their no-tool counterparts on both out-of-distribution benchmarks (e.g., Qwen3.5-27B: +4.0 points on Agentic-MME and +10.3 points on VTC-Bench). This demonstrates that our models do not merely memorize domain-specific tool-calling patterns, but instead acquire a transferable skill of knowing \emph{when} and \emph{how} to invoke visual tools, which readily extends to novel tasks and visual contexts.

\subsection{Ablation: Double Rejection Sampling}
\label{sec:ablation_gain}
\label{sec:notool_traj}
% Two distinct claims live in one table; keep them as two paragraphs.

% \paragraph{Setup.}
% All variants train Qwen3.5-9B on the same queries for a fixed budget (1{,}971 steps), isolating the retention rule: full \dataset (correctness $\cap$ positive tool-use gain), correctness-only, tool-use-gain-only, and a no-tool-trajectory SFT.
To validate our filtering strategy and confirm that fine-tuning gains stem from learned tool-use capability rather than naive SFT on hard queries, we compare four data variants under controlled conditions. All variants train Qwen3.5-9B on the same query set for a fixed budget (1{,}971 steps), differing only in the retention rule: (1)~full \dataset{} (correctness $\cap$ positive tool-use gain), (2)~correctness-only, (3)~tool-use-gain-only, and (4)~no-tool-trajectory SFT (trajectories without any tool invocation). Full results are reported in Table~\ref{tab:ablation}.

% \paragraph{Tool-use supervision beats text-only supervision.}
% Every tool-use variant outperforms the no-tool-trajectory SFT on average (\dataset $45.8$ vs.\ $37.1$, $+8.7$), so the gain comes from the \emph{tools}, not merely from SFT on these (hard) queries.
\paragraph{Gains originate from tool-use, not from SFT alone.}
% Every tool-augmented variant outperforms the no-tool-trajectory SFT on average (\dataset{}: $45.8$ vs.\ no-tool: $37.1$, $+8.7$), confirming that the improvements are attributable to learned tool invocation rather than simply training on additional difficult queries.
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ablation}, every tool-augmented variant substantially outperforms the no-tool-trajectory SFT baseline (+8.7 points on average), confirming that improvements stem from learned tool invocation rather than simply training on additional difficult queries.



% \paragraph{The filtering strategy matters.}
% The intersection dominates either filter alone: $45.8$ (both) $>44.1$ (correctness-only) $>42.4$ (tool-use-gain-only), with the widest margins on Table and Visual Search --- evidence that the correctness filter and the tool-use gain filter are complementary (correctness removes invalid demos; tool-use gain removes causally useless ones).
\paragraph{Both filters are complementary and necessary.}
Intersecting both filters consistently dominates either filter in isolation, with correctness-only and gain-only trailing by 1.7 and 3.4 points, respectively. The two filters address distinct failure modes: correctness filtering removes invalid demonstrations that would teach erroneous behavior, while the tool-use gain filter discards trajectories where tools are invoked but contribute no causal benefit. Their combination yields the highest-quality training signal. Full per-domain breakdowns are provided in Table~\ref{tab:ablation}.

\subsection{Cross-Domain Transfer}
\label{sec:cross_domain}
% Folded in from the former Discussion section.

% \paragraph{Setup.}
We fine-tune Qwen3.5-9B on each single-domain slice of \dataset{} separately
and evaluate on all five domains. Full results are in Table~\ref{tab:cross}.

\paragraph{Tool-use transfers as a domain-general meta-skill.}
Every single-domain model outperforms the base model with tools
(AVG 37.0--41.7 vs.\ 32.1), even on domains unseen during training.
For instance, GUI-only training lifts VisualSearch from 38.7 to 52.8 ($+14.1$)
and Table from 26.8 to 38.7 ($+11.9$), suggesting that certain tool-use
primitives---localized cropping, spatial grounding---are reusable across
visual reasoning tasks.

\paragraph{Transfer is asymmetric, with both positive and negative effects.}
Not all domains export equally well: GUI-only achieves the highest
single-domain average (41.7), while Web2HTML-only yields the lowest (37.0).
Moreover, we observe significant \emph{negative} transfer when tool-calling
strategies conflict: VisualSearch-only drops Web2HTML to 21.6
(vs.\ 42.0 base), as its ``repeatedly crop small regions'' pattern
interferes with the full-page rendering required by Web2HTML.
This reveals that over-specialized tool-use habits learned from a single
domain can actively harm incompatible domains.

\paragraph{The full mixture resolves strategy conflicts.}
Full \dataset{} dominates every single-domain variant on average
(45.8 vs.\ best single-domain 41.7, $+4.1$) and is the top performer
on four of five domains. By exposing the model to diverse tool-use
patterns, multi-domain training enables \emph{context-conditional}
tool selection---the model learns not just how to use tools, but
\emph{which} strategy to deploy given the visual input.

\paragraph{The GUI exception.}
GUI is the only domain where a single-domain model (36.3) outperforms the
full mixture (31.6). Under the point-in-bbox metric, a single precise click
suffices; non-GUI data dilutes this direct-imitation signal with multi-step
crop$\to$OCR patterns. We view this as a pointer to domain-adaptive mixture
weighting rather than a failure of tool-use supervision
(\dataset{} still improves GUI from 24.8 to 31.6 over the untuned base).
